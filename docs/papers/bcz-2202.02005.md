# BC-Z (arXiv 2202.02005): explained

**Title:** BC-Z: Zero-Shot Task Generalization with Robotic Imitation Learning
**Authors:** Eric Jang, Alex Irpan, Mohi Khansari, Daniel Kappler, Frederik Ebert, Corey Lynch, Sergey Levine, Chelsea Finn (Google / X), CoRL 2021 · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.02005) · [project](https://sites.google.com/view/bc-z/)

## 1. The problem
Can a vision-based imitation policy do **tasks it was never shown on the robot**, given only a language instruction or a *human video* of the task? And what kind of data collection gets you there?

## 2. Method
- **Data:** 25.9k robot demos (about 125 robot-hours, 100 tasks, 9 skills, 12 robots) plus 18.7k human videos of the same tasks.
- **HG-DAgger collection:** the operator watches the policy and takes over *only when it's about to fail*. Those interventions become training data. Over half the data is interventions.
- **Policy:** one RGB head-camera image goes into a ResNet-18 with **FiLM** conditioning on a 512-D task embedding. MLP heads predict delta xyz, delta rotation, and gripper at 10 Hz.
- **Task embedding:** either a frozen sentence encoder on the instruction, or a **video encoder trained to match the language embedding** of the same task. Without that alignment, video embeddings clustered by objects instead of by task.
- **Adaptive targets:** each action targets a state N steps ahead, with N chosen adaptively. This mattered a lot (45% vs. 3%).

## 3. Results
- **Unseen tasks (language):** 32% average over 29 held-out tasks, or 44% over the 24 that worked at all. With **human video conditioning** it was only 4%.
- **Multi-task beats single-task:** on one target task, the multi-task policy reached 52%, against 5% for a single-task policy trained on 1,000 demos.
- **Interventions beat plain demos at equal data:** 53% vs. 27% on a single task, and 47% vs. 23% on 8 tasks.
- **Intervention rate tracks success,** so it works as a live quality metric during collection.

## 4. Limitations
- Zero-shot success varies a lot from task to task. Many failures reach for the right object and then miss.
- Human-video conditioning works much worse than language.
- Instructions are simple verb–noun phrases.
- **The last centimeter of grasping and releasing** is the main failure mode, which is a low-level control problem.
- The data budget is large (125 robot-hours plus fleet teleop).

## 5. How it relates to your SO-101 project
- **Learning from failures by human intervention.** HG-DAgger is the imitation-learning version of "learn from real mistakes": a human corrects the policy at the failure point. Your failure-guided loop does the same thing *without* a teleoperator: real failures get rebuilt in MuJoCo and RL fixes them. Cite it as related work and as a possible baseline ("intervention-based correction"). Their finding that interventions are worth about 2× as much as fresh demos supports focusing your scarce real trials on failures.
- **Multi-task helps single tasks** (52% vs. 5%). This supports training one goal-conditioned policy across task variations even if the demo is only Task 1.
- **Video conditioning from human demos was weak** when the embedding came from the whole video. That's an argument for your *object-centric* use of human demos (tracking object poses and points, as in Track2Act and Dex4D) over learning a video embedding.
- **The last-centimeter failures** match OmniTacTune's "last mile" point. Expect these on SO-101 too, especially with no force sensing.
